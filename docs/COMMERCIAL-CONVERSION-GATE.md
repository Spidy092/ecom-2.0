# Commercial Conversion Gate — First 10 Paying Customers

**Status:** active product/business gate  
**Objective:** make the first 10 unrelated customers choose to pay for the product and successfully launch/use it.  
**Rule:** differentiation is useful only when it improves a purchase decision, customer outcome, support cost, or defensibility. Being different is not itself a goal.

## 1. The commercial problem

A new grocery WooCommerce product enters a market where buyers can already choose established products with years of reviews, updates, support history, demos, and marketplace trust.

That means a new product has a trust deficit even if its code or UI is better.

The product therefore has to make a buyer answer these questions quickly:

1. What exactly is this product?
2. Is it specifically suitable for my grocery/food store or my client?
3. What do I get after paying?
4. Can I make a credible store quickly?
5. Does it work with current WordPress and WooCommerce versions?
6. Will it be easy to customize without breaking the shopping experience?
7. What happens when WooCommerce changes?
8. What support and updates do I get?
9. Is the price justified versus established alternatives?
10. Can I trust the seller enough to use this on a real store?

If the sales site, live demo, documentation, setup experience, and product package do not answer those questions, more storefront features are unlikely to fix conversion.

## 2. Buyer groups for the first 10 sales

Primary launch buyers:

- grocery/local-food store owners building or replacing a WooCommerce storefront;
- WordPress freelancers building a grocery/food store for a client;
- small agencies that need a reliable starting point without a large required plugin stack.

Do not optimize V1 for every ecommerce niche.

## 3. The product package a buyer must understand

The initial paid package should be communicated as one coherent product:

- commercial block-first WordPress theme;
- companion storefront core plugin for product-owned functionality;
- one polished Modern Grocery starter store;
- guided installation/setup/import path;
- current WordPress/WooCommerce/PHP compatibility information;
- documentation and getting-started path;
- updates while the license/subscription is active;
- standard support while the license/subscription is active;
- changelog and security reporting route;
- clear license, refund, privacy, and support terms.

Do not sell the architecture as a folder list. Sell the outcome: get a credible grocery store live and keep it maintainable.

## 4. 30-second purchase-understanding test

Show the sales page to a target buyer for no more than 30 seconds, then hide it.

Without prompting, ask:

- What is the product?
- Who is it for?
- What is the main reason to choose it?
- What is included?
- What would you expect to pay?
- What question would stop you from buying today?

Gate target before public paid launch:

- at least 4 of 5 relevant buyers correctly describe the product and target customer;
- at least 4 of 5 can name one concrete purchase reason without repeating a vague adjective such as "modern" or "clean";
- no repeated critical unknown about installation, compatibility, license, support, update delivery, or refund policy remains unanswered on the sales site.

This is comprehension evidence, not proof of conversion.

## 5. Willingness-to-pay test

The current pricing hypothesis is US$59/year for one production site.

Do not validate this by asking only "does $59 sound okay?"

For each target buyer:

1. show the live demo and product package;
2. show the actual price and terms;
3. ask whether they would buy for a real upcoming project;
4. if no, capture the primary reason before discussing discounts;
5. categorize the objection:
   - trust/reputation;
   - missing capability;
   - setup effort;
   - customization concern;
   - performance concern;
   - compatibility concern;
   - support/update concern;
   - license/price concern;
   - product not relevant;
   - other.

Milestone A remains: one unrelated customer actually pays. Stated willingness is useful research but not a substitute for payment.

## 6. What the live demo must prove

The demo is not a visual gallery. It is the strongest product proof before purchase.

A buyer should be able to verify:

- search and browse are understandable;
- quantity shopping is fast and obvious;
- Cart state remains visible;
- delivery-area checking has a clear role;
- mobile shopping is first-class;
- product pages, cart and checkout still behave like a credible WooCommerce store;
- the storefront feels polished enough for a real business, not an engineering prototype;
- desktop adaptation is complete;
- there are no broken placeholder states, fake social proof, fake urgency, or incomplete demo sections.

One exceptional demo is more valuable to V1 than several weak demos.

## 7. What the setup proof must show

A buyer also buys implementation confidence.

Before public paid launch provide:

- installation instructions;
- minimum requirements;
- exact required/recommended plugins;
- starter-store import/setup flow;
- what the importer changes;
- how to set logo/colors/type;
- how to connect WooCommerce pages;
- how to configure delivery/serviceability behavior that the product exposes;
- how to update theme/core safely;
- how to recover from a failed import/update;
- system-status/help route.

Commercial acceptance target:

A relevant WordPress freelancer or store owner should be able to reach a credible starter storefront from a fresh supported WordPress install without developer intervention. Record time, questions, failed steps, and documentation lookups.

## 8. Trust assets required before payment

A new seller cannot borrow the trust of products with thousands of sales. We have to manufacture trust through evidence and clarity.

Required trust assets:

- live demo;
- real product screenshots at mobile and desktop widths;
- compatibility matrix;
- public changelog;
- documentation;
- support policy;
- refund policy;
- license explanation;
- security reporting page;
- performance claims only with reproducible methodology;
- accessibility claims only for behavior actually tested;
- clear product update history once releases exist.

Never use fake purchase counters, fake testimonials, fake ratings, or fake scarcity.

## 9. Familiarity versus originality

Use familiar ecommerce conventions when they reduce buyer/shopper learning cost.

Examples:

- `Add`, `Cart`, `Search`, `Saved for later`, account and checkout should be immediately recognizable;
- common WooCommerce expectations should remain compatible unless a tested product improvement justifies changing them.

Use original product expression only where it creates useful value, clearer hierarchy, brand recognition, or a defensible workflow.

Do not reject a familiar pattern merely because a competitor uses it. Do not copy a competitor's distinctive branded expression, assets, code, or layout.

Commercial decision rule:

> Familiar where familiarity improves comprehension. Better where evidence shows a problem. Original where originality strengthens the product. Never different just to be different.

## 10. Purchase reasons we need to prove

Current hypotheses — not yet validated claims:

1. **Grocery-first workflow:** the product is designed around multi-item everyday shopping rather than a generic catalog demo.
2. **Faster credible launch:** one polished starter store plus guided setup can reduce the work required to reach a presentable grocery storefront.
3. **Smaller required stack:** WooCommerce + product core, without mandatory Elementor, may reduce builder/plugin burden.
4. **Maintainability:** public/supported WordPress and WooCommerce surfaces plus compatibility testing can reduce upgrade anxiety.
5. **Mobile shopping quality:** quantity, browse, search, delivery and Cart behavior are designed as one mobile-first system.
6. **Commercial clarity:** documentation, updates, support and compatibility are treated as part of the product, not after-launch extras.

Every one of these must move from hypothesis -> evidence before being marketed as a strong superiority claim.

## 11. Competitive purchase test

For each serious buyer study, show the same buyer:

- our live product/demo;
- at least two relevant established grocery WooCommerce alternatives;
- WoodMart as a broad market/trust benchmark when useful.

Use the same scenario and avoid coaching.

Ask:

- Which would you shortlist for a real project?
- Why?
- What creates trust?
- What creates doubt?
- What would make you switch your choice?
- Which feels easier to launch and maintain?
- Which product package is easiest to understand?

We do not need every buyer to choose us. We need to know exactly why relevant buyers do or do not choose us.

## 12. Commercial build priority

After the current storefront visual/reliability gate, prioritize work in this order unless new evidence changes it:

1. buyer/store-owner validation of the product/package;
2. one polished Modern Grocery demo;
3. zero-confusion install/setup/import/system-status flow;
4. documentation/getting-started and compatibility matrix;
5. licensing/update delivery/checkout proof;
6. sales site with clear product, demo, package, price, support, refund and trust information;
7. first paid beta/customer acquisition;
8. support/objection logging and product iteration.

Broad new shopper-facing feature families remain lower priority unless they remove a repeated purchase blocker.

## 13. Commercial release gate

Do not call V1 commercially ready until all of these are true:

- [ ] one polished live Modern Grocery demo exists;
- [ ] sales page passes the 30-second comprehension test with target buyers;
- [ ] setup has been observed with relevant store owners/builders;
- [ ] compatibility requirements are explicit and tested;
- [ ] documentation/getting-started is usable;
- [ ] license/update/support/refund terms are explicit;
- [ ] checkout/update-delivery path works;
- [ ] first-buyer objections have been recorded and synthesized;
- [ ] no marketing claim exceeds the available evidence;
- [ ] one unrelated customer has paid or the product remains an unpaid beta/validation product.

## 14. Success metric

The V1 success metric is not uniqueness, feature count, demo count, GitHub activity, or social attention.

It is:

> The first 10 relevant, unrelated customers choose to pay, can launch/use the product successfully, and give us enough evidence to know what should be improved next.
